#### The three offers

**Offer A**

> We found your profile and would love to have you start immediately. The role is
> general administrative support, 20 hours a week, paid at a rate well above the
> market. We will need you to complete a short paid onboarding package first —
> the cost is refunded with your first payment. Please reply on Telegram, we do
> not use the platform's messaging.

**Offer B**

> Thanks for your application. Before we talk, could you complete this 6-hour
> exercise: research 50 prospective clients for us and build a contact list. We
> will review it and get back to you. This is a normal part of our hiring process.

**Offer C**

> Hello — I run a small design studio and need help with my inbox and invoicing,
> about 5 hours a week to start. I have attached the contract; the rate is on page 2. Happy to start with a two-week trial at the same rate. Do you have a
> reference I could contact, and can we do a short call this week?

#### What to produce

For each offer, a short audit:

```text
Offer A
Patterns:      above-market rate; urgency; moved off-platform; onboarding fee
Disqualifying: "complete a short paid onboarding package first" — paying for work
Verdict:       do not proceed
```

Then the three-line message ending the worst one.

#### How it will be judged

- **Offer A contains three disqualifying requests.** Paying to get the job, moving
  off the platform, and a rate that is the bait. Naming all three is the point.
- **Offer B is the hardest case.** It is not obviously fraud — but an unpaid
  six-hour deliverable that the client can use is free labour, whatever it is
  called. The professional response is not a refusal; it is a counter-offer.
- **Offer C is legitimate**, and saying so matters. An exercise that taught you to
  see fraud everywhere would leave you unable to work. Note what makes it normal:
  a described scope, a written rate, a trial, a reference request, a call.
- **The closing message is short and final.** No lecture, no accusation, no
  apology.
