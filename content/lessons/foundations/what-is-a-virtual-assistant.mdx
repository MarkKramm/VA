A **virtual assistant** is someone who does administrative, organisational or
professional support work for a business, remotely, usually as an independent
contractor or a freelancer.

That definition hides more than it explains, so it is worth unpacking.

## The work falls into four broad categories

**Administrative and organisational work.** Calendar management, inbox
handling, scheduling appointments, maintaining documents and client records,
following up on things. This is the largest category and usually the easiest
place to start.

**Communication on someone else’s behalf.** Writing and replying to email,
handling chat, drafting messages that a client sends under their own name. This
work carries a client’s reputation, which is why tone and accuracy matter so
much.

**Data and research.** Entering and cleaning data, building spreadsheets,
compiling information from the web into a structured format, producing reports.

**Specialist support.** Customer service, social media scheduling, design work,
video editing, bookkeeping assistance, website maintenance, or building automations
between a client’s tools. These usually require prior experience in that
specialism — you are hired for the speciality, not for being a VA.

Most people start in the first two and specialise later, if at all. That is
normal and it is not a smaller career.

## Who you work for changes the job more than the tasks do

This is the part that surprises new virtual assistants, and it matters more
than which software you know.

**A direct client** is a business owner or small team who hires you themselves.
You may be the only support person, so the range of tasks is wide and so is the
responsibility. Boundaries matter enormously, because there is no manager
between you and the client.

**An agency** resells your time to its own clients, usually in defined blocks of
hours. The work is more structured and usually lower-risk, because the agency
handles scoping and client management. The trade-off is less autonomy and often
a lower ceiling on what you can charge.

**An employer** hires you as an employee, with a salary, set hours, benefits and
supervision. This is the least common arrangement for the role, and the most
stable when it exists.

**Self-employed** means you find your own clients, set your own rates, and carry
the costs and the risk yourself. The independence is real, and so is the part
where you have to find work before you have any.

## What the role is not

It is not a stepping stone to a "real" job, and anyone who tells you otherwise is
selling something. It is also not passive: the hours are real, the deadlines are
real, and the standard of work is judged by whether a busy person can rely on
you.

## A note on what this platform will not tell you

You will read a great deal about rates, demand and earnings in this field. Be
suspicious of anyone who presents specific figures as settled fact. Rates vary
enormously by specialisation, client size, country and whether you are working
under an agency or directly — and anyone quoting one number as _the_ number is
either oversimplifying or selling. Where a figure genuinely matters, this
platform will either cite a source or tell you to treat it as an estimate.
