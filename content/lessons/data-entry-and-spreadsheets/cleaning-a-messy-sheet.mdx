Cleaning data is not tidying. It is making a series of decisions about what is
true, and writing down the ones you made. A cleaned sheet with no record of the
cleaning is a sheet nobody can trust.

## Duplicates, and why you rarely delete them

Two rows can look identical and still be two real things — two people with the
same name, two invoices for the same amount, two orders placed minutes apart.

The safe routine:

1. **Copy the sheet** before you touch it. Always.
2. **Sort by the column you suspect, then scan visually.** Spreadsheets have a
   built-in duplicate highlighter, but it only finds exact matches — it will miss
   `Acme Co` and `Acme Co.` and `Acme  Co` (two spaces).
3. **Standardise first, then look for duplicates.** Removing stray spaces and
   fixing capitalisation turns near-duplicates into exact ones, which are far
   easier to review.
4. **Mark, do not delete.** Add a column `Duplicate?` with `yes` and a short note.
   The client decides what to remove; you show them what you found.

That last step is the professional one. Deleting a row that turned out to be a
separate order is not recoverable by an apology.

## Standardising formats

Pick one format per column and apply it everywhere. The three that cause the most
trouble:

- **Dates.** Use one unambiguous format for anything you create — `YYYY-MM-DD`
  sorts correctly and cannot be misread. Where the source is ambiguous
  (`01/02/2026`), do not guess: flag the column and ask, because getting it wrong
  changes the meaning of the data.
- **Names and companies.** Decide on capitalisation and spacing, then apply it.
  `TRIM` removes leading, trailing and doubled spaces. Be careful with
  accents and non-Latin characters — standardising those away destroys real
  information.
- **Numbers.** Remove thousands separators before summing, and check whether a
  column is currency or a plain number. Mixed formats make `SUM` return nothing
  and look like a broken sheet.

## Making the work reversible

Two habits make everything you do undoable:

- **One change type per copy.** `Sheet (cleaned)`, `Sheet (deduped)` — so a
  problem can be traced to the step that caused it.
- **A change log**, four lines long, at the top of the file or in a separate
  sheet:

```text
2026-10-05  Standardised all dates to YYYY-MM-DD (412 rows).
2026-10-05  Trimmed whitespace in Company column. 12 values changed.
2026-10-05  Flagged 7 suspected duplicate rows in column H; not removed.
2026-10-05  Left 3 rows with unreadable dates untouched — listed in Notes.
```

If you cannot say what you changed, you have not finished cleaning — you have
just made the sheet different.

## What not to do

Do not delete rows to make a count match. Do not "fix" a value by guessing what it
should have been. Do not clean a column the client did not ask you to touch,
however tempting it looks. Do not remove accents, leading zeros in reference
numbers, or anything else that might be meaningful.

## Practice

Take a list of twenty names and companies, deliberately introduce a few near
duplicates and inconsistent capitalisations, then clean it using the routine
above. Finish by writing the four-line change log.
