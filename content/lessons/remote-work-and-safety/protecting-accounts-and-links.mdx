A VA is given access to things a client would not give a stranger: email, banking
dashboards, social accounts, shared drives. That access is the job, and holding it
safely is a professional skill rather than a technical one.

## Holding a client's credentials

- **Use a password manager.** Every serious one has a shared or emergency-access
  feature. Credentials go in there, never in a chat message, a document, a
  spreadsheet, or your notes app.
- **Never send a password in the same channel as the username.** If a client sends
  you a password by email, move it into the manager and tell them you have, and
  suggest they change it now that it has travelled by email.
- **Never reuse a password** across accounts, and never reuse your own password
  for a client's account.
- **Turn on two-factor authentication** wherever the client allows it, and prefer
  an authenticator app or a hardware key over SMS.
- **Use your own account where you can.** Many tools let a client invite you as a
  user, which is safer and more auditable than everyone sharing one login. Suggest
  it — it protects them, not just you.
- **Remove your own access when the work ends.** Then tell the client you have.
  Access that nobody remembers is access that outlives the relationship.

## Recognising phishing and unsafe links

Phishing is not usually a badly spelled email from a stranger. The common shapes:

- **A message that creates urgency.** "Your account will be closed today." Real
  providers rarely work to a same-hour deadline by email.
- **A link whose text and destination differ.** Hover, or long-press on a phone,
  and read the actual domain. `paypal.com.secure-login.example` is not PayPal —
  read the part immediately before the first single slash.
- **A login page you reached by clicking.** If you need to sign in to something,
  navigate to it yourself from a bookmark rather than following a link.
- **An unexpected attachment.** An invoice you were not expecting, a `.zip` or a
  document that asks you to "enable macros" to view it. Do not open it; ask the
  sender by a different channel.
- **A request that changes payment details.** Treat any change of bank details as
  fraud until confirmed by a voice call to a number you already had.
- **A sign-in prompt you did not trigger.** A two-factor code that arrives when
  you are not logging in means somebody has your password. Change it immediately.

## If something goes wrong

Speed matters more than blame, and the order is:

1. **Change the password** on the affected account, then on any account that
   shared it.
2. **Sign out other sessions** if the provider offers it.
3. **Tell the client**, plainly and immediately: what you noticed, when, what you
   have done. Hiding it for a day while you investigate is the decision that turns
   an incident into a breach of trust.
4. **Check for rules that were added** — mail forwarding, recovery addresses,
   payment details. Attackers leave doors open.
5. **Write down what happened** while you remember it.

Do not be afraid to report a suspected compromise that turns out to be nothing.
The cost of a false alarm is a short conversation; the cost of a silent breach is
the relationship.

## What not to do

Do not store credentials in a browser's basic password store on a shared computer.
Do not accept a login from a link in a message, however convincing. Do not share a
client's credentials with anyone, including another VA, without the client's
explicit instruction. Do not reuse a client password you have seen elsewhere.

## Practice

Take three messages from your own inbox — real ones — and check each for the six
phishing shapes above. Then write the message you would send a client if a
two-factor code arrived that you had not requested.
