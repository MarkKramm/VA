Fraud aimed at people looking for remote work is common, and it is aimed at people
who need the work — which is exactly when judgement is hardest. The defence is not
suspicion of everything; it is knowing the small number of patterns that almost
never appear in legitimate work.

## The patterns that recur

No single one of these proves fraud. Two or three together is a strong signal.

- **Hiring without any real conversation.** An offer after two messages, no
  interview, no questions about your experience.
- **Far above the going rate for the task.** The number is the bait. If a routine
  administrative role is being offered at several times what the work is worth,
  ask why.
- **Vague about the work, specific about the money.** They cannot describe the
  tasks, but they can describe the payment.
- **Moved off the platform immediately.** A real client on a freelance platform
  keeps the contract there, because it protects both sides. "Let's talk on
  Telegram instead" is usually about leaving the platform's protection.
- **Pressure and urgency.** Start today, decide now, limited slots. Legitimate
  work rarely expires in an hour.
- **Poorly written, but not in the way you would expect.** Not bad English —
  copied boilerplate, inconsistent job titles, a company name used in several
  different forms.
- **A company that does not exist.** No website, no registration, no address, a
  domain registered weeks ago, or a website that is a template with placeholder
  text.
- **Recruiters using free email addresses** for a company that clearly has its own
  domain.

## Requests that end the conversation

Any one of these should stop the process immediately. You do not need to be polite
about it.

- **Paying to get the job.** Onboarding fees, training fees, a "security deposit",
  a fee to unlock work, a cost for software or equipment you must buy from them.
  Real employers pay you; they do not charge you.
- **Receiving and forwarding money.** A "payment processing" or "funds
  transfer" role where money arrives in your account and you send it on. This is
  money laundering, and it makes you the person the bank investigates.
- **Using your own bank account for the client's money.** Related, and the same
  answer.
- **Buying gift cards or vouchers** as part of any task. There is no legitimate
  reason for this, ever.
- **Sending identity documents before there is a contract** — passport, national
  ID, a photo of yourself holding ID. These are used to open accounts in your
  name.
- **Installing software they send you**, or giving remote access to your computer,
  before you have a signed agreement and a verified client.
- **A "test task" that is real work.** Small paid tests are normal; a substantial
  unpaid deliverable that the client can use is free labour.
- **Being asked for your passwords**, or to log in to a site they link to.

## Checking before you work

Fifteen minutes of checking, before you invest hours:

1. **Search the company name** with the word "scam" and separately with "review".
   Fraud is usually reported by the previous people who applied.
2. **Find the company's own website** and look for a registration number, a real
   address, and a person's name. Then check the registration with the relevant
   official registry if the country publishes one.
3. **Check the domain age.** A company claiming years of trading on a domain
   registered last month is not a company claiming years of trading.
4. **Find a named human.** Search the recruiter's name and see whether they exist
   anywhere other than this conversation.
5. **Ask a specific question about the work.** A real client can answer it. A
   fraud cannot, and will usually respond with more urgency.
6. **Reverse-image search a profile photo.** Stolen photographs are common.

Write down what you found before you decide. A decision made after thirty seconds
of research is a decision made on the fraud's terms.

## If you have already engaged

Stop, do not send anything further, and:

- **Contact your bank immediately** if you shared account details or moved money.
- **Change passwords** for anything you entered on a link they sent.
- **Tell the platform** you found the work on, if any.
- **Report it** to the relevant national fraud reporting service for your country
  and, if the client claimed to be elsewhere, theirs.
- **Do not blame yourself.** These operations are designed by people who do this
  full time. The useful response is to write down what you saw so you recognise it
  faster next time.

## What not to do

Do not accept an offer because the money solves a problem this month — that is the
mechanism. Do not send identity documents, bank details or software access before
a verified contract exists. Do not let a client's urgency set your pace: a genuine
deadline survives you taking an hour to check.

## Practice

Take a real or invented job offer and score it against the eight patterns and the
eight disqualifying requests above. Then write the three-line message you would
send to end the conversation.
