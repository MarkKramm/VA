The difficult part of time management in VA work is not speed. It is that three
clients each think their request is the urgent one, and you are the only person who
can see all three at once.

## Ranking requests by consequence

Do not rank by who asked first, and never by who asked loudest — that rewards
panic and trains clients to panic. Rank by what happens if it is late:

1. **Something breaks or someone is blocked.** A client cannot invoice because
   they are waiting on your figure. A meeting cannot happen because the invitation
   is missing. Do these first.
2. **A deadline today that you agreed to.** You said it would be done; it is the
   promise that matters, not the size of the task.
3. **Work that unblocks other work.** A task that lets three more tasks proceed
   beats a task that is merely urgent.
4. **Everything else**, in the order it arrived.

Two questions sort almost every conflict: _"What breaks if this is late?"_ and
_"Who else is waiting on it?"_ If the answer to both is "nothing" and "nobody",
it goes at the bottom, whatever the subject line says.

When two clients genuinely conflict, say so. "I have a deadline for another
client this morning; I can do this by 14:00 — does that work?" is a professional
message. Silently doing both badly is not.

## Planning a day you can finish

- **Estimate in blocks, not hours.** "One hour" is a fiction; "one 45-minute block
  with a break after" is a plan.
- **Plan no more than about two thirds of the day.** The remaining third is what
  absorbs the messages that arrive, the file that will not open, the call that
  overruns. A day planned to the minute fails at the first interruption.
- **Do the hardest thing first**, before the inbox. Concentration is highest at
  the start and the inbox is designed to consume it.
- **Write the plan down.** A plan held in your head is a plan you cannot compare
  against what actually happened.

At the end of the day, note what you did not finish and why. That note is how you
learn that your estimates are optimistic, which is more useful than any
productivity method.

## Protecting the work that needs concentration

- **Batch the shallow work.** Answering messages in two or three passes a day
  rather than continuously is faster in total and less tiring.
- **Turn notifications off while you do the hard thing.** Not muted — off. Muted
  notifications are still read.
- **Say when you are unavailable.** "I'm heads-down on the report until 13:00, then
  I'll be on messages" prevents a client wondering why you are quiet.
- **Protect a real break.** Working through lunch is how an afternoon is lost.

## What not to do

Do not accept a task without knowing when it is due — an undated task becomes
urgent at the worst moment. Do not multitask between clients; switching costs more
than the work. Do not treat "busy" as "productive": a day of messages and no
finished work is a day that produced nothing a client can use.

## Practice

List everything you have to do tomorrow, then rank it by consequence using the two
questions above. Then plan two thirds of the day around the top of that list, and
leave the rest unplanned on purpose.
