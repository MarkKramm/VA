Remote work fails in two directions. Either the client cannot tell whether you are
working, or you are working at midnight and calling it commitment. Both are
avoided by the same thing: agreeing how you work before you start.

## Agreeing how you work

Five things, agreed in writing in the first week, prevent most later friction:

| Question                      | Why it matters                                                                                          |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Which channels, for what?     | A client who sends an urgent task by a channel you check twice a week will conclude you are unreliable. |
| What response time is normal? | "Within one working day" is a commitment you can keep. Silence is a commitment you cannot.              |
| What hours are you reachable? | Availability is not the same as working hours. State both.                                              |
| What is an urgent request?    | Without a definition, everything becomes urgent.                                                        |
| How do I ask a question?      | One channel, batched, is better than four messages across a day.                                        |

Put it in one short message and ask the client to confirm. That message is the
reference point for every later conversation about response times.

## Being visible without being noisy

The client's fear in remote work is that nothing is happening. The fix is not
constant updates — it is **predictable** ones:

- **At the start**: "Working on the invoice reconciliation today; I'll send it
  before I finish."
- **When something changes**: immediately, with the consequence and your proposal.
- **At the end**: one line. "Done — reconciliation attached. Two supplier
  statements were missing; noted in the file."
- **Weekly, if the work is ongoing**: a short list of what moved.

A daily message that says "still working on it" adds nothing and trains the client
to ignore your updates. A message that says what changed is the one they read.

## Holding a boundary

A boundary is not a confrontation. The pattern is: acknowledge, state the
constraint, offer a workable alternative.

> **Client (22:40):** Can you get this to me for the morning?
>
> **You (next morning, 08:15):** I saw this after my working hours. I can have it
> to you by 11:00 today, or if it is genuinely urgent for the morning, message the
> urgent channel and I'll pick it up next time — shall we agree on what counts as
> urgent?

That is professional, it is not a refusal of the work, and it moves the
conversation to the rule rather than the incident. What it does not do is apologise
for having hours.

Three boundaries worth setting early: a response window, a channel for genuine
emergencies, and what you do not do (say, calls before a stated time). None of them
need justifying with a reason — "I'm not available then" is a complete answer.

## What not to do

Do not reply instantly at all hours for the first week to look keen — that becomes
the standard you are held to. Do not ignore a message that arrives outside your
hours; answer it at the start of the next working period. Do not accept an
unreasonable request silently and then resent it: the client cannot see your
resentment, only the delay.

## Practice

Write the five-line "how we work together" message you would send a new client,
covering channels, response time, hours, what counts as urgent, and how to ask a
question.
