Finding a page is the easy half. The half that earns a client's trust is being
able to say, honestly, whether the page is right.

## Who says so, and would they know

Three questions, in order:

1. **Who published this?** A named organisation, a named author, or nothing at
   all. "Nothing at all" is an answer.
2. **Why would they know?** A tax authority knows tax rules. A blog post about
   tax rules knows what someone read on a tax authority's website.
3. **When?** For anything that changes — rules, prices, software, contact
   details — a date is part of the claim. An undated page about a changing fact
   is a page you cannot use.

If a claim survives all three, it is worth passing on. If it fails one, say so in
your write-up rather than quietly dropping it.

## Primary sources and secondhand ones

A **primary source** is the thing itself: the company's own contact page, the
regulator's own guidance, the document you were asked about. A **secondhand
source** is a page describing the thing: a news article, a summary, a listicle.

Secondhand sources are useful for finding the primary one, and for context. They
are a poor place to stop, because every retelling loses detail and adds
confidence. When a client asks for a fact, get to the primary source and cite
that.

Two practical moves:

- **Follow the link.** If an article cites a report, open the report. Often the
  article is accurate and the report says something slightly different.
- **Look for the same fact in two independent places.** Two articles that both
  copied the same press release are one source, not two.

## When you cannot verify something

The most valuable sentence in research is: _"I could not confirm this, and here is
what I found instead."_

A client would rather have an honest gap than a confident wrong answer, because a
wrong answer gets acted on. If a figure appears only in an undated blog post,
write that down. If two sources disagree, show both and say they disagree — do not
average them, and do not pick the one that reads better.

## What not to do

Do not treat a search result's summary as the source. Do not treat repetition as
confirmation. Do not present an old figure as current because the page is still
online. Do not fill a gap with something plausible — plausible is exactly what a
client cannot check.

## Practice

Take three claims from a topic you know well and score each one against the three
questions above. You will usually find that at least one widely repeated claim
fails the "when" test.
