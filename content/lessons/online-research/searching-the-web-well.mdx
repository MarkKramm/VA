Most people search the way they talk. That works for simple questions and fails
for the questions clients actually ask, because a client's request arrives with
their context attached and a search engine has none of it.

## Turning a request into a query

A client writes: _"Can you find out if there are any rules about how long we have
to keep customer invoices in the UK?"_

Searching that sentence returns articles about invoicing. The words that matter
are four: **invoice retention period UK**. The rest is context you already have.

The habit is to strip the request down to its nouns before you type:

| What the client said                                           | What you search                               |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| "Is there anything about how long we keep invoices in the UK?" | `invoice retention period UK`                 |
| "Who runs that packaging company we liked?"                    | `packaging company` + the town or the product |
| "What's the going rate for this?"                              | `[service] pricing` or `[service] rates`      |

If the first search fails, the fix is almost always a more specific noun, not a
longer sentence.

## The operators worth remembering

You do not need a list of thirty operators. Four cover nearly everything:

- **Quotes** — `"exact phrase"` finds the words in that order. Use it for names,
  error messages and legal wording.
- **`site:`** — `site:gov.uk invoice retention` searches one site only. This is
  the single most useful operator in client work, because it lets you go straight
  to the authoritative source instead of the article about it.
- **Minus** — `jaguar -car` removes a meaning you do not want. Useful for
  company names that collide with something famous.
- **A year or a date range** — `invoice retention 2026` biases results toward
  recent pages, which matters for anything that changes.

Quotes and `site:` are the two to learn first. Between them they solve most
research tasks that involve a fact rather than an opinion.

## Reading results before clicking

The results page is information. Before opening anything, look at:

1. **The domain.** `gov.uk`, a company's own site and a professional body are
   different kinds of source from a content farm. Decide what kind you need
   before you click.
2. **The date.** A page about tax rules from four years ago may be worse than
   useless. If no date is shown, that is itself a signal.
3. **The snippet.** If the sentence you need is already visible, you may not
   need the page at all — but you still need to open it to check the context.

## What not to do

Do not click the first result and stop. Do not accept an answer from a page that
does not say who wrote it or when. Do not search for the answer you expect to
find — if you already know what you want the answer to be, you will find a page
that agrees with you, and that is not research.

## Practice

Take a request you have been given recently, or make one up, and write down the
four or five words you would actually type. Then write the `site:` query you
would use to go straight to the authoritative source.
