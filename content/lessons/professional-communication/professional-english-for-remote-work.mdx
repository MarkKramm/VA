Most unclear business English is not a grammar problem. It is a habit problem:
long sentences, indirect requests, and phrases that were copied from someone
else's email because they sounded professional.

## Register, without sounding stiff

Professional written English sits between a text message and a legal document. The
test is not "is this formal?" but "would a competent colleague write this?"

| Too casual           | About right                                     | Too stiff                                                             |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| hey, done that thing | I've finished the March reconciliation.         | I wish to inform you that the aforementioned task has been concluded. |
| can't do it          | I can't make Thursday — would Friday work?      | Regrettably, the requested timeline cannot be accommodated.           |
| send it over asap    | Could you send the file when you have a moment? | Kindly furnish the document at your earliest convenience.             |

Contractions are fine. "Please" and "thank you" are fine. Long Latin words are
usually not — they make a sentence harder to read without making it more polite.

## Sentences that survive a quick read

Three habits do most of the work:

1. **One idea per sentence.** If you have written "and" three times, you have three
   sentences.
2. **Active voice for anything you did.** "I've updated the sheet" beats "the
   sheet has been updated", because it says who did it and reads faster.
3. **Concrete nouns.** "The invoice" is clearer than "the document in question".

Read your message out loud once. Anywhere you run out of breath is a sentence to
split.

## Constructions that read badly

- **The indirect request.** "It might be good if we could perhaps look at the
  timeline" reads as uncertainty. Say: "Can we review the timeline this week?"
- **The bare imperative.** "Send me the file." Add one word: "Could you send me
  the file?" The meaning is identical and the tone is not.
- **The hedge stack.** "I think maybe it could possibly be an issue" — pick one
  hedge or none. Two reads as evasive.
- **The apology opener.** Starting with "Sorry to bother you" sets your work up as
  an imposition. Start with the content.
- **Idioms and humour.** They do not survive translation, and the client may not
  share your culture. A dry joke that lands with a colleague can read as
  unprofessional to a client in another country.

## What not to do

Do not use a thesaurus to sound more professional. Do not translate a formal
phrase from your first language literally — if you are unsure, write the simple
version; simple English is never read as unprofessional, and unclear English
always is.

## Practice

Take three sentences from your own writing and rewrite each one to be shorter
while keeping the meaning identical. Then rewrite one request so it is answerable
with a single word.
