The failure mode of a shared inbox is not a full inbox. It is a message that four
people read, three assumed someone else would answer, and nobody did.

## One owner per message

Every message needs exactly one person responsible for it at any moment. Two
mechanisms make that visible:

- **Assignment.** Assign the message to yourself when you start it. An unassigned
  message in a shared inbox is nobody's.
- **Status.** "Open", "waiting on customer", "waiting on us", "done". The status
  that matters most is **waiting on us** — that is the list of things the team owes
  someone, and it should be short and reviewed daily.

A useful rule: never leave a message in the shared inbox without either an
assignment or a status. Ten seconds of housekeeping prevents a client waiting a
week.

## Replying in a team

Before you reply, scroll to the bottom of the thread. In a shared inbox, the most
recent message is often from a colleague, not the client, and answering the
client's original question again makes the team look disorganised.

- **Reply to the client, not to the team**, when the answer is for the client.
- **Use internal notes** for anything the client should not read — "this is the
  third time they have asked, I've flagged it" belongs in a note, not a reply.
- **Do not sign as someone else.** Sign with your own name, or with the team's
  name if that is the convention — but never attribute a message to a colleague
  who did not write it.
- **If a colleague is mid-reply**, leave it. A second answer arriving five minutes
  later reads as chaos.

## Complaints and escalations

A complaint is not a personal attack and it is not yours to absorb silently. The
pattern:

1. **Acknowledge quickly.** Within the working day, even if you cannot fix it yet.
   "I've seen this and I'm looking into it — I'll come back to you today."
2. **Do not promise what you cannot deliver.** "It will be fixed by Friday" is a
   commitment; "I'll find out and tell you when" is not, and is usually better.
3. **Escalate on a rule, not on a feeling.** Agree with your client in advance
   what counts as an escalation — a refund request, a legal threat, anything from
   a named person — so the decision is not made under pressure at the time.
4. **Write down what you did.** An escalation with no record is one you will have
   to reconstruct later.

## What not to do

Do not answer a complaint defensively, even when the client is wrong. Do not leave
a message unassigned because you are not sure whose it is — assign it to yourself
and ask. Do not use the shared inbox as a chat channel; it is the client's view of
your team.

## Practice

Take five messages from a shared-inbox scenario and write, for each: who owns it,
what status it carries, and whether the next reply is public or internal. Then
write the one-line acknowledgement you would send for the complaint among them.
