Triage is not reading. It is deciding. The goal of a triage pass is that every
message has been **dealt with once** — acted on, scheduled, filed, or answered —
so that the inbox only ever contains things you have not yet looked at.

## The four decisions

For each message, make one of four decisions and move on. Speed matters more than
perfection; you can change your mind later, and a message you have not decided
about is the only real problem.

1. **Do it now, if it takes under two minutes.** Replying to confirm a time,
   forwarding one file, correcting an obvious typo. Doing it immediately is
   cheaper than remembering it.
2. **Turn it into a task.** It needs real work, so it leaves the inbox and becomes
   a line on a list with a date. The inbox is the wrong place to store work.
3. **File it.** It needs nothing from you, but you may want to find it later.
   Archive it, or apply a label and archive it.
4. **Delete it.** Newsletters you will not read, notifications you have already
   seen. There is no prize for keeping them.

If you cannot decide, it is almost always a task — and the task is "decide what to
do about this".

## Archive is not delete

Archiving removes a message from the inbox and keeps it, searchable, forever.
Deleting throws it away. In client work the default should be archive, because you
will be asked "what did they say about the deadline?" months later.

Two habits that make archive useful rather than a second pile:

- **Labels for projects or clients**, not for actions. "Acme Co" is a label.
  "To do" is not — that is what your task list is for.
- **Search, do not scroll.** Every mail client searches full text. A message you
  archived is findable; a message you left in the inbox is findable too, and also
  in your way.

## Tracking what you are waiting for

The messages that cause real problems are the ones where the ball is in someone
else's court and you forget about it.

Keep a short list — a note, a task, a spreadsheet, whichever you will actually
check — of **what you sent, to whom, and when you expect an answer**. Then your
follow-up is a decision, not a memory. A follow-up sent after three working days
is normal; a follow-up sent after three weeks looks careless.

## What not to do

Do not read an email and leave it unread as a reminder — that is what your task
list is for, and it makes the unread count meaningless. Do not use the inbox as
your filing system. Do not mark something read when it still needs work.

## Practice

Take twenty messages from an inbox — yours, or a set someone shares — and triage
them out loud, saying which of the four decisions you are making and why. Do not
open anything that can be decided from the subject line and first two lines.
