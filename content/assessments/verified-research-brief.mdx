id: verified-research-brief
title: Produce a Verified Research Brief
summary: >-
  Answer a real client question from primary sources and hand over a short written
  brief that says what you found, where it came from, and what you could not
  confirm.
purpose: >-
  Proves you can research to a standard a client can act on: the right source, the
  uncertainty stated, and the answer in the first line.
difficulty: beginner
estimatedMinutes: 60
skills:
  - research-search
  - research-verification
  - research-reporting
prerequisites:
  - id: verifying-what-you-find
    reason: The brief is judged on whether the sources behind each claim are the right kind of source.
  - id: writing-a-research-brief
    reason: The deliverable is the brief that lesson teaches you to write.
scenario: >-
  A client is considering working with a supplier in another country and asks you
  three questions: is the company registered, how long has it been trading, and
  what is its registered address. They add: "just send me what you find, I don't
  need an essay." You have an hour. One of the three answers will be harder to
  confirm than the others, and the client will not know which.
requirements:
  - Every claim has a source attached to it, not to the end of the document.
  - The answer comes first, in one or two sentences.
  - Anything you could not confirm is stated, with what you would check next.
  - The brief is under 250 words.
  - At least one source is a primary source — the register, the company itself, or an official publication.
instructions:
  - Pick a real company you can research, or invent one with a plausible web presence.
  - Write the three answers, and note the kind of source behind each one.
  - Look for the answer that is hardest to confirm, and say so honestly.
  - Attach one link per claim, next to the claim.
  - Close with a short uncertainty line.
deliverable: >-
  A brief of under 250 words, with the three answers, a source attached to each
  claim, and a closing statement of what you could not confirm.
evaluationCriteria:
  - id: answer-first
    description: The first line answers the question rather than describing your method.
  - id: primary-source
    description: At least one claim rests on a primary source, and the brief says which kind each source is.
  - id: source-per-claim
    description: Every claim has its own source next to it, and no claim is unsourced.
  - id: uncertainty-stated
    description: The hardest-to-confirm answer is identified as such, with what you would check next.
  - id: length
    description: The brief is under 250 words and contains no padding or restatement.
hints:
  - Start with the register, not a search engine. Searching for a company name returns pages about the company; the register returns the record.
  - If two sources agree, check whether one copied the other before treating it as confirmation.
  - The address is usually the easiest and the trading history the hardest. Say which one gave you trouble.
  - Write the uncertainty line last, and do not soften it into a footnote.
commonMistakes:
  - Describing the search process instead of the findings, which makes the client do the work again.
  - Citing a directory site as though it were a register.
  - Quietly omitting the question you could not answer, which is worse than answering it with a gap.
  - Attaching sources in a list at the end, so the reader has to match them up.
referenceGuidance: >-
  A good brief reads like a colleague answering a question in a corridor: the
  answer first, three supporting facts, the sources attached, and one honest line
  about what is still open. If a reader has to reach the second paragraph to learn
  the answer, the brief is in the wrong order.
